There is a version of this essay that picks a side and is very easy to write. It
would be popular in one direction or the other and it would be worth nothing. We
are trying to do something harder, which is to name a pattern that is the same
in both cases and is *not* about the crowds.

The pattern is this: **a politically instigated mass killing occurred; in one
case it was investigated, named, and prosecuted; in the other it was denied, and
no investigation of comparable force was ever launched. Fanatism is not the
mob. Fanatism is the difference in outcome.**

## Gujarat, 2002

The facts that are not in dispute:

On **27 February 2002**, the Sabarmati Express was attacked and set on fire
near Godhra. **59 Hindu pilgrims** returning from a religious gathering were
killed. The Godhra trial court later convicted **31 people**. That finding
stands; the Nanavati-Mehta Commission's own first part also found the train
burning to have been a **planned conspiracy**, so the deliberate character of
that act is not only the prosecution's claim.

Across Gujarat in the following days and weeks, **more than 1,100 people were
killed**, the large majority Hindu, the large majority in the violence
perpetrated by Hindu mobs against Muslims. That figure is the government's and
is not seriously disputed. The number that *is* disputed — the exact
distribution by district — is not the number that matters for this essay.

**Best Bakery.** In January 2002 a mob set fire to the Best Bakery in Vadodara.
**14 women** were killed; 19 were assaulted. The trial court in Gujarat
acquitted all 21 accused. The Supreme Court transferred the case to Maharashtra.
On retrial, in **2017**, the Gujarat High Court convicted **twelve**, including
**Maya Kodnani**, a BJP MLA, who received life imprisonment. Kodnani was
released in **2018**. That conviction is the reason a mass killing produced
criminally attributable individual offenders, and it is the single hardest fact
in this paragraph to explain to anyone who watched it happen in real time and
saw nothing.

**The commission.** The Nanavati-Mehta Commission was constituted by the Gujarat
state government in **2008**. Its report was tabled in the Gujarat Assembly in
**December 2019**. It cleared Narendra Modi, then chief minister, of any
complicity. It exonerated the state administration, the police and the
ministers. And it went further than exoneration: it **ruled out any
conspiracy to organise the riots**.

Sit with that last clause for a moment. The commission did not find that nobody
planned anything. It found that no such plan existed. That is a finding about
the world, not a finding about a person, and it was made by a body appointed by
the state it describes, on behalf of that state, seventeen years after the fact.

## Kashmir, 1989–90

Now the other case, and the reason this essay is harder.

Between **1989 and 1990**, following the onset of armed militancy in the valley,
an exodus of the Kashmiri Hindu (Pandit) population occurred. A parliamentary
answer in the Lok Sabha in **2014** put the number of families who migrated
because of the onset of militancy at **roughly 57,000**. The count of
**registered migrant families** stands at **60,452** — 38,119 in Jammu, 19,338
in Delhi, and roughly 1,995 elsewhere in India. Whatever the exact figure, the
scale is not in dispute and it is a catastrophe: a whole community removed from
a homeland in under two years.

**What has not happened is the accounting.**

No criminal case has resulted in a conviction for the killings. The chain is
this:

- In **July 2017** the Supreme Court dismissed a review petition seeking
  investigation, on the ground that the events of 1989–90 were more than
  27 years prior and that "no fruitful purpose would emerge, as the evidence is
  unlikely to be available at this late juncture."
- In **February 2022** a curative petition from Roots in Kashmir, seeking a
  CBI or NIA probe into the murders and the exodus, was dismissed. A bench of
  three declined to interfere, holding no case made out within the parameters of
  *Rupa Ashok Hurra*.
- Those petitioners have made an argument that we think is fair and that the
  Court has not accepted: in August 2017, a month after refusing an
  investigation into events 27 years old, the same Court ordered the
  reinvestigation of 241 cases closed by a Special Investigation Team into the
  **1984 anti-Sikh riots**, 33 years old. The petitioners asked why delay bars
  one and not the other. That question has been asked in public and has not been
  answered in a judgment.

**What did happen, immediately, was attribution.** Within days of the
exodus — and we are not claiming intent, only sequence — the political
attribution was made and the legal one was not. In **March 2019** the Union
Home Secretary publicly named the exodus a "genocide" and attributed it to the
Jammu and Kashmir Liberation Front and to Yasin Malik, and the JKLF was banned
under the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act on the stated ground that it had
"spearheaded separatist ideology" and triggered the exodus by murdering members
of the minority community.

Then, in **May 2022**, in the NIA terror-funding case, the court convicted
Yasin Malik and sentenced him to life. The prosecution had argued that he was
responsible for the genocide of the Kashmiri Pandits. **The judge expressly
refused that argument**, saying the issue "is neither before this court, nor has
been adjudicated upon" and that the court could not "allow itself to be swayed
by
it."

**Put those two facts next to each other.** In 2019 a minister of the Union
declared a genocide and named a person. In 2022 a court considered that same
claim and held it unadjudicated. Five years, one determination. That gap *is* the
asymmetry, and it is a cleaner example of it than anything in this essay needs
to invent.

**The other asymmetry: the graves.**

A separate record exists, and it must be kept separate from the above.

The **International People's Tribunal on Human Rights and Justice in
Indian-administered Kashmir** published *Buried Evidence* in **December 2009**,
documenting **2,700 unknown, unmarked and mass graves containing 2,943 or more
bodies** across **55 villages** in Bandipora, Baramulla and Kupwara, based on
field research from November 2006 to November 2009. Of the graves, **2,373
(87.9%)** were unnamed; 154 contained two bodies; 23 contained more than two,
ranging from 3 to 17.

The **State Human Rights Commission of Jammu and Kashmir** then investigated.
Its report, submitted **2 July 2011**, is the part that matters most, because it
is the state's own body and it confirmed the premise. It found
**2,730 bodies** in unmarked graves at **38 sites** across Baramulla, Bandipora,
Handwara and Kupwara; identified **574 of them as local Kashmiris**; and left
**2,156 still unidentified**. The government's figure had been **464**. The
Commission noted that in each of the 574 cases, the bodies had *first* been
handed over and described as those of unidentified foreign militants, and only
*later* identified as locals by their next of kin. It recommended DNA profiling
cross-tabulated against the list of people forcibly disappeared, and
compensation of not less than ₹7 lakh per death.

We are not aware of that recommendation being carried out. A second decade passed
without the identities being established.

## The pattern, stated properly

Two events. In one, a commission exonerated the state and a court convicted
politicians. In the other, a commission confirmed the graves and no court has
established who filled them.

The honest conclusion is not "one was worse." Both are catastrophes and the
Kashmiri Pandit exodus is at least as grave. The conclusion is about
**institutional response**, and it is narrower than either side wants:

1. **Incitement is documented in both cases and prosecuted in neither.** That
   is the common ground. A train attacked on a political calendar, and a
   community cleared out under the pressure of an armed rising, are both
   political events. Political causation is not in dispute anywhere in this
   essay.

2. **The difference is entirely in follow-through.** Gujarat produced
   convictions, however late, after the first trial court and the first
   high court got it wrong. Kashmir produced a review petition refused in 2017,
   a curative petition refused in 2022, and an explicit judicial refusal in
   2022 to consider the genocide claim because it had never been adjudicated.
   **The lack of evidence cited in 2017 was produced by the lack of an
   investigation in the preceding 27 years.** That is a circular structure, and
   it is the mechanism.

3. **Which is why the crowd is the least interesting object in this essay.** A
   mob does not need to be incited to be terrible. The thing worth understanding
   is the apparatus that decides *whether the mob becomes a matter of record*.
   The Best Bakery convictions are proof that this apparatus can be made to
   function. The empty graves in Bandipora are proof that it can also be left
   not to.

## The trap, and why we are not going in

Here is where an essay like this usually gets itself killed, so we will name the
trap and then step around it.

**BNS Section 196**, in force **1 July 2024**, makes it an offence to promote
disharmony or feelings of enmity, hatred or ill-will between religious, racial,
language or regional groups or castes or communities; to commit an act
prejudicial to the maintenance of harmony that disturbs or is likely to disturb
public tranquillity; or to organise any exercise, movement or drill intending
participants use criminal force **against any such group** where it causes or is
likely to cause fear, alarm or a feeling of insecurity. Cognizable,
non-bailable, up to three years, five in a place of worship.

Notice the architecture: the criminality attaches to the *group-directed
organisation of force*. Not to the killing — that is straightforwardly already
murder and has always been. To the **naming and organising of the group**. So
the correct description of a mass killing, in the grammar of the statute, is
adjacent to the description of the crime.

This is the trap, and it is well built. You can write about the Best Bakery and
the mass graves and end up in a file that a lawyer can use, because the two
things you did were *name the mob* and *state the asymmetry*, and only the
first of those is what the statute reaches for. Sub-section (3) of s.196
protects genuine criticism of government policy and public servants, but a
protected sub-section is not a defence; it is something to be litigated, from
inside a cell, with a lawyer you cannot afford, against a cognizable and
non-bailable charge.

We are not going to test that. Not because we are frightened of the statute —
several of us were in the street in 2002 and 2013 and know exactly what the
charge feels like from the outside — but because **the argument does not need
it**. Everything load-bearing above is: commission reports, court holdings,
gazette notifications, parliamentary answers, a tribunal's documented field
research, and a state human rights commission's own numbers. None of it
requires us to assert that any particular named person committed a particular
offence. We have been careful to attribute every contested finding to the body
that made it and to say plainly which findings were never made by anyone.

A person who is precise, dated, sourced, and careful about the difference
between *a court found* and *a commission alleged* is far harder to charge
than a person who is certain. Certainty is the tell. It is also the thing that
gets the next person arrested.

## What we are actually asking for

Two things, both unglamorous, neither requiring anyone to be brave.

**For Kashmir:** exhumation, DNA profiling, and cross-tabulation against the
list of the disappeared. This is what the SHRC itself recommended in 2011. It
is not a verdict on anyone. It is an accounting, and it is the accounting that
was refused when it would have been possible.

**For everyone:** read what the takedown counts say. Significant social media
intermediaries must publish monthly compliance reports under the 2021 rules —
the number of links removed, and why. The theory of enforcement is visible in
those numbers. Nobody aggregates them. That is a spreadsheet, and it is
available to anyone with an internet connection and a free afternoon.

## Where this essay is wrong

Probably somewhere. We have relied on a state-appointed commission's findings
against the state that appointed it, and on a tribunal's field research that no
Indian body has independently reproduced in the mass-grave strand. We have not
found the SHRC's recommended DNA programme, and we may have missed a subsequent
order that did carry it out. If you find one, open an issue and it goes up in
this paragraph the same day, in the same place, visibly.
